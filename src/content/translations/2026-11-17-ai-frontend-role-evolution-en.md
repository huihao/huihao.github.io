Start with the conclusion: **the front-end job wasn't killed off — it was taken apart and reassigned.**

The work didn't vanish; it changed hands. Boilerplate, assembled UI and repetitive tests went to the model. Setting standards, enforcing acceptance and signing off when something breaks moved out of "done along the way" and became a job of its own — and one that no longer belongs to front-end engineers alone.

That conclusion doesn't come from panic. It comes from what the big companies wrote down this year. Read a handful of key posts and one deep-dive report side by side and the major Chinese tech firms are doing three things at the same time:

1. **Writing AI into the delivery process**, and no longer treating it as a toy but as capacity;
2. **Turning team experience into something agents can read**, moving it from "something a person remembers" to "rules an agent executes";
3. **Erasing the "frontend / backend" job category from the org chart.**

Three consecutive moves on the same line. Once AI pushes the cost of writing code close to zero, organisations recomputed why they should divide people by tech stack — and the answer came back: no longer needed.

I am not going to summarise the year's industry news here. I want to settle one thing: what the front-end role has been turned into, and where a person's value now sits inside it.

---

## 1. Three things happening at the same time

Start with the hard numbers. Every judgement below rests on them.

**Meituan's Business R&D Platform** put it plainly in a 7 May 2026 post, ["Using an Agent-evaluation mindset to manage AI coding — the practice of an AI-driven refactor across 310,000 lines of code"](https://tech.meituan.com/2026/05/07/Agent-AI-Coding.html): **more than 90% of the team's code is generated by AI**; the system carrying this AI-coding practice grew from under 50,000 lines in June 2025 to **310,000 lines**; the team ships **16 requirements per month** on average (80% business requirements, 20% technical ones); and headcount **tripled within a year**.

Read those numbers against each other. Code volume grew more than sixfold, headcount grew threefold, and delivery stayed steady month over month.

**LatePost** laid out the cross-company picture in a 30 June 2026 report, ["AI wrote 90% of the code: the painful moment for big-company programmers"](https://www.163.com/dy/article/L0N76P0I0531M1CO.html) (the link here is the NetEase syndication): Chinese big companies' AI code rate was **only 20–30% in the second half of last year, and reached 90% at the highest this year**; Google announced in April that **75% of new code is written by AI**; and according to Anthropic's own report, as of May 2026, **more than 80% of the code merged into its main repository was written and submitted independently by Claude** — a number that was in the single digits in early 2025.

**ByteDance's technical VP Hong Dingkun** gave a same-definition set of figures at a public event on 26 June 2026: over the past year ByteDance's **AI code contribution rate grew more than sixfold, AI-coding token consumption grew fivefold, and the AI code merge rate grew more than twofold**. The merge rate is the one to watch — it measures the share of AI-written code that, after human correction, actually lands on the trunk. That number is doubling.

There is an even more basic number, and it is about cost. Tencent's Hunyuan Hy4 flash shipped on 4 November 2026 with 224B parameters and 12B active; a third-party deep review published on the Tencent Cloud Developer Community on 16 November puts the **cost of a single agent task below 0.3 yuan** and calls it the start of "the two-fen cache era". The same review contains a line that is easy to skip: **"one cost item people forget: human review."**

Three cents per task, ten cents per review. That is no longer a question of "is AI any good". It is the arithmetic of "do we still split work by headcount".

Then two events with a stronger signal:

- In the autumn of 2026, a screenshot of an internal notice began circulating across multiple accounts. Its content: **"With the development of AI coding, the company has decided that from now on technical positions will no longer be divided by tech stack; all technical positions will uniformly be called Agent engineers."** The screenshot came from a Feishu message by former Alibaba P10 Bi Xuan, who has since left to found a startup. **It is not an official Alibaba announcement**, but during its circulation window it was discussed by a number of accounts.
- On 12 November 2026, at Tencent's 28th-anniversary company livestream, Ma Huateng raised **AI-Native workforce restructuring** (as relayed by the WeChat account *AI Night Reading, 11.14*).

From numbers to people. The LatePost report names three:

- A front-end engineer with ten years at Fliggy received a generous severance package on 11 June, and half the desks in the work area were empty;
- A woman engineer at Shopee who had topped the performance ranking year after year was notified on 8 June;
- Zhao Shu, a merchant-side front-end developer at a Shanghai company, was given a blunt reason for leaving: **"the back end can now write front-end code by itself."** In her last month she had no work at all; afterwards she started learning Python to round out her full-stack profile, and when she interviewed, almost every question was about AI. She rated her chances at "70 per cent".

There is also Gao Hong, a front-end engineer at Shopee who interviewed for an "agent engineer" role and came away with: **"I kept finding that the interviewers often knew less than I did."**

So "will AI replace front-end engineers?" stopped being an open question in 2026. Part of the work has already changed hands, and faster than most people prepared for.

The real question is: **if the job title itself is being erased, what exactly is being erased — the role, or the role as we imagined it?**

My answer is the latter. The front-end role we imagine is a craft defined by *slicing images / components / integration / styling*. What the big companies are actually cancelling in 2026 is **the division of labour by craft itself**.

## 2. What gets cut first is the boilerplate, not the skill

One confusion worth clearing up first: **what AI took over is not "front-end skill". It is the slice of front-end work that is precisely specified, precisely accepted, and automatically detectable when wrong.**

Alibaba's Taobao Tmall Technology team summarised this in March 2026 in *"AI coding in front-end practice: a retrospective"*: AI is markedly efficient at **boilerplate code, unit tests and UI components**, but still limited on complex business scenarios. That matches what every team on the ground reports — what AI can write is "the things already spelled out in the requirements document".

Break a front-end engineer's day open and it looks roughly like this:

| A day's work | Who does it | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Slice the page from the design, tune spacing, write the static states | **AI takes over** | Precisely specified, enumerable styles, and a screenshot shows the mistake |
| Scaffold CRUD forms, list pages, modals | **AI takes over** | Highly repetitive patterns, mostly boilerplate |
| Write unit tests, add type annotations, generate API docs | **AI takes over** | There is an obvious acceptor — does the suite pass |
| Wire up data against the API doc, write mocks | **AI takes over** | Inputs and outputs are both written down |
| Debate with design about whether an interaction makes sense | **Human** | Requires judging intent, not translating intent |
| Pick between several options and state the trade-off | **Human** | Requires owning the consequence |
| After release, work out whether the bug is in the code or in the requirement | **Human** | Responsibility cannot be outsourced |

That table lines up with Meituan's numbers: **more than 90% of code is AI-generated, yet 100% of requirements are still covered by developers doubling as testers (RD as QA)**, with a Human-in-the-loop testing SOP attached (Meituan A1, 7 May 2026).

In other words, **fewer people produce code; the same number of people verify it.**

In the *2026 Enterprise AI Programming Practice Handbook* published by ByteDance's TRAE team in March 2026, the front-end section is not about "how to make the model write more like a human". It is about **Code Connect — establishing a mapping between design files and code** — and about connecting external systems through the MCP protocol. The real engineering effort in front end is moving from "writing markup" to "feeding design intent, context and business rules to the agent".

The model side is closing the same gap. Doubao 2.0 explicitly emphasises "enhanced front-end semantic understanding and CSS layout reasoning". Once general-purpose models start optimising specifically for those two things, the threshold for "being able to write the interface" will not hold for long.

## 3. Where the saved time goes

This is the part that matches what people actually feel on the ground, and it is the exact opposite of the "AI makes life easier" story.

One line from the LatePost interviews recurred throughout:

> **"The efficiency gains from AI did not turn into leisure. Work that used to take ten days, the boss now expects delivered in one."**

And one more, colder:

> **"Being the one who uses AI to replace someone else is treated as the default reason you get to stay."**

The mechanism is not complicated: **the unit of assessment did not change; the amount of work per unit time did.**

The machinery around it is remarkably tidy:

- One ByteDance team **put the AI code rate into its OKR** and displays it as a rolling counter on a website;
- At Alibaba's Taobao Tmall Group, senior management pushed a unified dashboard where **every individual's AI usage data is visible to everyone else**;
- Meituan asked every team to map out "which work processes can be standardised and which steps can be distilled into skills";
- According to a Meituan veteran quoted in the LatePost report, after management pushed this top-down, the company's AI code rate went **from 10% to 70–80%**, and a lot of people got it running within two weeks. Wang Xing's remark after meeting AI people is quoted repeatedly: **"The water has risen… whether you are struggling in it or not, you all have to learn to swim."**

So the feeling is not "there is less work". It is that **far more has been packed into the same working hours**. A front-end developer used to ship two pages a week; now the expectation is two pages, plus a dashboard, plus a regression pass.

That is also why the efficiency dividend never became a benefit: **the dividend was collected by the organisation, and it was never the employee's to distribute.**

He Chuan, a Coding Agent developer inside Alibaba, describes his situation as an "internal battle royale" in which P7s, P8s and P9s each fight their own tier. He says: **"Everyone wants a place of their own in the AI era, but the likeliest outcome is that you don't have one."**

It sounds pessimistic, but it names a genuinely new mechanism: people are now judged not on how much they wrote, but on how much the team produced and how far the result can be trusted.

## 4. Three turning points of the role

Sorting through what the big companies wrote this year, three turning points hold up.

### Turning point one: the tech-stack category is abolished — the role shifts from a division of labour to a unit of responsibility

The most direct evidence is the circulating screenshot: **"From now on technical positions will no longer be divided by tech stack; all technical positions will uniformly be called Agent engineers."**

In the same period, a number of workplace-media accounts carried the same story: Ant, JD, Meituan and Dewu had, one by one, dissolved or merged their standalone front-end departments; JD removed the "front-end engineer" hiring keyword; Meituan folded its front-end team into the back end. These need careful labelling:

- According to the essay *"Code Traitors and the Industrial Revolution"*, Dewu's dissolution of its independent front-end department happened in **March 2025**; November 2026 is the renewed reaction to it, and **Dewu has officially responded**;
- The claim that "Ant removed the job title 'front-end engineer' from the organisation in July (2026-07)" comes from workplace media, **not an official announcement**;
- These are **rumours and retellings**, and should not be written as established fact.

Dewu is the interesting case. On 13 November 2026 the account "Dewu Tech" published *"Stress-testing AI agents: governance and evaluation tuning in practice"* on the Tencent Cloud Developer Community — a company that reportedly folded front end into an AI full-stack team, and the first technical thing it chose to write about publicly is how to evaluate what an agent produces.

The direction is not hard to read. The LatePost interviews put it more precisely:

> **"Role boundaries are dissolving — front end is being merged into back end, and newly posted roles are simply renamed 'full stack'."**

The question is why this is happening now.

The reason for dividing labour was never the technology itself. It was **to reduce communication cost and learning cost**. Letting a front-end developer write a back-end endpoint used to cost "three months of learning plus cross-stack communication friction"; AI has pushed that migration cost close to zero — which is precisely what the circulating screenshot means by "a front-end engineer no longer needs three months to learn how to write a back-end endpoint". Add the three-cent cost per agent task and "sending one person off to learn another stack" stops being a line item worth defending in most teams.

But what probably pushed this over the threshold was not the technology. It was the arithmetic. Huxiu's 13 August 2026 piece, ["The real turning point of the AI revolution: job compression is starting to outrun job creation"](https://www.huxiu.com/article/4882798.html) (published with permission from the WeChat account *HavenlonLabs*), takes apart a premise that rarely gets said out loud:

> **"Industry expansion usually means organisational expansion."**

After old jobs disappear there was always a larger pool of new employment to absorb people — that is why previous technological revolutions were painful without ever making "work" itself disappear. AI is challenging that premise:

> **"A business process that used to need 20 people might in future be 5 people plus a set of agents."**
>
> **"For the first time, company size may grow sharply while headcount barely grows."**

On that arithmetic, dropping the split by tech stack stops being one boss's whim and becomes a shortage-of-people-not-jobs calculation: the business is still growing, it just does not need that many people any more, so there is no reason to keep dividing them the old way.

Once migration cost approaches zero, **the benefit of division (saved communication and learning cost) disappears while its friction cost (hand-offs, waiting, boundary disputes) remains or even grows**. So organisations naturally change the axis of division: from "by language and directory" to "by business domain and delivery outcome".

Going from front end to full stack is therefore not "learning one more language". It is **redefining the role from a list of skills to a list of responsibilities**. You are no longer on this team because you know Vue; you are here because you own the outcome of this business domain.

### Turning point two: code review and acceptance become the new bottleneck — human value moves from "writing" to "proving"

Easiest to overlook, and possibly the most important of the three.

Meituan's Basic Technology department wrote three sentences in its 29 October 2026 post, ["Using AI coding in a system that cannot afford mistakes: practice and boundaries in distributed storage"](https://tech.meituan.com/2026/10/29/AI-Coding-IN-Distributed-Storage-System.html), each of which can stand as a conclusion on its own:

> **"AI moved the bottleneck from 'writing' to 'verification'."**
>
> **"AI's mistakes are not in the code, but outside the code."**
>
> **"Fluency is not evidence. Consistency is not correctness. Green is not resolution."**

"**Fluency is not evidence**" is worth reading twice by anyone who writes code. A model that walked a flow end to end, a suite that went from red to green, code that looks tidy — none of these constitute correctness. It may in fact be wrong.

What Meituan's team actually did is worth copying: a whole-repository review surfaced **117 suspected issues**, of which **36 were confirmed by red tests, 3 were disproven, 41 confirmed by reading source, and 37 remained unverified** — note that last column, the 37 unresolved cases were not counted as results. Another, harder set of figures: **678,000 lines of WARN logs** were scanned over 11 days across 34 call sites, and the **5 defects that emerged were all real, with zero false positives**. The CMake overhaul covered **31 build files**.

"Verification" looks like this now: **not a glance at a diff, but the design of an evidence chain that can force an error into the open.**

Meituan's argument found an echo in its peers the following month. Dewu Tech's piece on stress-testing AI agents opens on exactly this point:

> **"Once a smart analysis agent is connected to real business, a result that drifts off course may mean that anything in the chain broke — evidence collection, tool invocation, rule validation or model inference. Looking only at the final conclusion makes it hard to locate the root cause, and impossible to verify whether a change worked."**

On 17 November the Tencent Cloud Developer Community also carried *"You've seen all of this before: look at the harness from another angle and it all clicks"*, with a very concrete case: a long-running agent task that took 40 minutes and handed back **a beautifully formatted analysis report that was entirely wrong**. The post-mortem found three things braided together — at minute 12 a tool call threw an exception and a single `except` swallowed it; the agent never got a result but kept writing, treating stale data from the previous round as success; and there was no third-party review, so the artefact went straight into the delivery directory.

> **The dashboards stayed green. Not one alert fired. Not one error was raised.**

In the same week, *"One agent or many: from task structure to engineering trade-offs"* made a point I like a lot: **count agents by "decision units" rather than by "number of model calls"** — a single agent can call tools in parallel, and more models or more role names does not equal more agents.

Three different companies, one month, all writing about the same thing: **once output multiplies, the hard part moves from "make it run" to "prove it ran right".**

Which is why the conclusion in Meituan's A1 post is the centre of the whole article:

> **"When 90% of code is generated by AI, the focus of the team's work should shift from 'writing code' to 'designing and maintaining an engineering environment in which AI can reliably produce code'."**

The nature of code review changes with it: the value of human review shifts from "did you write it correctly" to **"are we solving the right problem under the right constraints"**. The first is error-checking; the second is boundary-setting.

Add Meituan's A2 principle: AI debugging can only act as a **"hypothesis generator"**, and root-cause attribution must be done by a human. And the layered authorisation path they designed: **read-only analysis → controlled modification → controlled execution → human decision** — the further right you go, the heavier the responsibility, and the less it can be outsourced.

The conclusion is blunt: **the most expensive skill in the AI era is not writing code. It is signing off on what the AI produced.**

### Turning point three: experience becomes an organisational asset

Once the job categories are abolished, organisations immediately fear one thing: **experience leaves with the people who hold it.**

Ten years of front-end experience means knowing every trap in the business domain: which field turns out to be dirty, which flow blew up three years ago, which component change detonates three other pages. All of that used to live inside a senior colleague's head; the moment roles merge and seniors leave, the capability leaves with them.

So the third thing the big companies did this year was **turn that experience into something an agent can read**:

- **Meituan** turned conventions into always-on **AI Rules + Skills + pre-review checks**, and framed the whole method as a move from "human-to-human alignment" to **"human-to-machine alignment"** (A1);
- **Alibaba**: Tmall built an **"AI full-stack R&D knowledge base"**, distilling business documentation and coding standards into assets the whole team can reuse, to solve the problem that general-purpose models do not understand business logic (Taobao Tmall Technology, March 2026); a second post the same month, *"AI coding: from tool efficiency to paradigm shift — what are we still missing?"*, names the bottleneck precisely: **"duplicated knowledge construction, and a hard time reducing cost"**;
- **ByteDance**: the TRAE handbook breaks down **context engineering**, with the front-end section using MCP to reach external systems and Code Connect to map design files to code;
- **Tencent**: on 11 March 2026 it launched **SkillHub**, "an AI skills community optimised for users in China" — productising skill packages and SOPs.

Even the working rhythm of front-end teams themselves has changed. Shouqianba's front-end team summarised a **usage SOP adapted to the front-end rhythm** in *"AI coding practice: from blind use to control"*. Meanwhile, Ctrip's flight-tickets front-end team wrote *"Why does your AI coding get stuck at Copilot?"* — and the answer is: **what blocks you is almost always context that has not been set up properly.**

Neither of those two is an official big-company document, but together they say more than the official ones would: **when "configuring the agent's context" becomes a daily chore, the core work of a front-end developer has already shifted from writing code to writing configuration.**

And with that, the value of the veteran front-end developer changes too: from "writes fast" to **"writes the rules well"**.

> **In the past your experience lived in your head. Now it has to be written as the agent's Rule.**

## 5. Five big companies, five answers

The same problem, five different answers. Put together, they are complete.

| Company | What it did this year | What it means for front-end roles |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Meituan** | 90% AI-generated code + an agent-evaluation system + layered authorisation (read-only analysis → controlled modification → controlled execution → human decision) + RD as QA (Meituan A1, A2) | The role shifts from "delivering code" to "designing and maintaining an engineering environment in which AI can reliably produce code"; the AI code rate goes straight into performance review |
| **Alibaba** | Tmall's AI full-stack delivery practice + AI full-stack R&D knowledge base + Qoder as an AI-native IDE (expert-team mode, "Leading Technology Award" at the 2026 World Internet Conference; the company says it serves 8 million users); Qianwen Office public beta on 2026-08-03 | The tool layer makes "one person, many stacks" the default; the asset layer turns business knowledge into shared property; management drives individual productivity through a unified dashboard |
| **ByteDance** | TRAE's *2026 Enterprise AI Programming Practice Handbook* (context engineering; MCP and Code Connect for front end); Doubao 2.0 strengthening front-end semantic understanding and CSS layout reasoning; Hong Dingkun disclosing AI code contribution up more than sixfold, tokens up fivefold, merge rate up more than twofold; 37,000 developers in the TRAE AI Creativity Contest; a 2026-11-03 internal rule restricting external AI tools from internal codebases | Models and tools are deliberately closing front-end's weak spots, while AI usage is simultaneously brought into performance review and governance boundaries |
| **Tencent** | Two product lines — WorkBuddy (an all-scenario AI office agent) and CodeBuddy (for software development), with Liu Yi as Tencent Cloud VP; 2026 Q1 active-user retention above 60% and paying-user retention above 80%; China Academy of Information and Communications Technology FDE validation in November 2026 plus an ecosystem-partner programme; the SkillHub skills community from 2026-03-11; CodeBuddy CN and Alibaba Cloud Bailian Coding Plan both moving to paid tiers | From "free tools for trials" to "priced per headcount, plus third-party certification, plus ecosystem revenue share" — AI capability now drives headcount and budget directly |

Side by side, the five paths are clearly different.

**Meituan** has gone deepest on "how do you manage code that AI wrote": from the agent-evaluation mindset to layered authorisation, from "aligning people" to **"aligning machines"**. Its definition of the role is remarkably complete — **what you design is not code, but an engineering environment in which AI can reliably produce code**.

**Alibaba** is pushing three lines at once: tools (Qoder as an AI-native IDE rather than "an IDE plus a plugin"), knowledge (Tmall's AI full-stack R&D knowledge base), and management (Taobao Tmall Group's unified dashboard where every individual's AI usage data is visible to everyone). Qoder's expert-team mode has a vivid pitch: **"one sentence summons 13 'programmers'"**.

**ByteDance** gives you three voices that have to be heard separately. The TRAE handbook is "we want to help you build front end faster". Hong Dingkun's figures are "we have already been doing this ourselves". And the internal rule of 3 November 2026 is "external tools are not allowed near our code". Put together, they are a complete organisational answer: **accelerate adoption while tightening the boundary**.

**Tencent** concentrates on the product side: turning AI programming into a commercial product with retention rates, payment, third-party certification and an ecosystem. One number is worth looking at: on desktop monthly visits, Tencent's WorkBuddy leads at 20.97 million, ByteDance's TRAE IDE at 12.79 million, and Alibaba's QoderWork at 7.88 million (The Paper, 2026-08-10). **The AI-native IDE is competing for the desktop entry point — and where the entry point sits eventually decides headcount.**

**Dewu** is the interesting sample. It is the only one of the five whose first public technical article after folding front end into an AI full-stack team is about **how to evaluate what an agent produces** — after the merge, the first gap that shows up is evaluation capability, not code capability.

As for Tencent's own front-end teams describing the change in their own words — **no official 2026 long-form post has been found**. But the articles that the Tencent Cloud Developer Community published in the month of November 2026 share a striking theme: Dewu Tech's *"Stress-testing AI agents: governance and evaluation tuning in practice"* (13 Nov), *"You've seen all of this before: look at the harness from another angle and it all clicks"* (17 Nov), *"One agent or many: from task structure to engineering trade-offs"* (17 Nov), plus a third-party deep review of Hunyuan Hy4 flash (16 Nov).

Not one of those four is about "how to write front end faster". All four are about "how to verify what an agent produced". That is good news for front end and bad news at the same time: good, because "verification" genuinely is valuable now; bad, because verification is no longer front end's exclusive territory.

## 6. The compressed part: pushed down, not gone

The compressed work has not vanished. It has moved.

- **Into the model**: boilerplate code, UI components, unit tests, API documentation;
- **Into the toolchain**: scaffolding, CI, build systems (Meituan's A2 CMake overhaul covered 31 build files);
- **Into the process**: conventions, acceptance, audit, permissions (Meituan's layered authorisation turns "am I allowed to change this" into a rule of the process).

Being pushed down is not the same as disappearing. Boilerplate code went from "typed by a human" to "generated by a rule", but **the rule is still written by a human, and a wrong rule is still a human's problem**. So the new skill is "writing rules", not "writing code".

There is something else, though, that deserves more attention than "pushing down": **what disappeared were jobs that never got created in the first place.**

The third chapter of that Huxiu piece is titled "the first to disappear are the jobs that were never supposed to exist". The scenario it sketches is almost comically plain: a company with 100 people expects business to grow 30% next year, so under the old productivity it should expand to 130; with AI in place, the existing 100 can carry the new business.

> **Statistically, nobody lost their job. In reality, 30 jobs that would have existed did not exist.**

No severance letter, no compensation, just one extremely calm management decision: no expansion this year.

An OECD survey of more than 5,000 small and medium-sized companies caught the early signal: 83% say generative AI has not yet changed their overall staffing needs; companies reporting **lower** staffing needs account for 9%, above the 6% reporting **higher** needs; and **14% say generative AI has reduced their reliance on external contractors**. That last figure lines up exactly with the earlier reporting on the shrinking pool of outsourced labour (New Weekly's *"A portrait of coders under the AI shock"*, syndicated by Sina Finance, 2026-11-05).

So here is an uncomfortable pattern: **the first to be compressed are always the people at the very bottom, with the least bargaining power** — and they never even receive the letter. They are the most direct victims of this "pushing down", and they carry a warning with them: **the order of descent is fixed; people near the top of the pyramid are not affected last, but they are not immune either.**

One level deeper, a 2025 study from Stanford's Digital Economy Lab on high-frequency ADP employment data produced a sharper set of numbers: in occupations with high AI exposure, the relative employment of early-career workers aged 22 to 25 fell by around **16%** after controlling for firm-level shocks, while senior workers in comparable occupations stayed relatively stable, with the decline concentrated in jobs where AI leans towards "automation" rather than "augmenting humans". The ILO's global youth employment report from August 2026 adds that roughly **6.1%** of the jobs held by 15-to-29-year-olds fall into the high-exposure category.

Put those two together and you get a conclusion that matters enormously for front end: **AI took away the first rung of the career ladder — and that rung is exactly how the industry replenishes itself.** For people already in the game, AI raises the value of seniority. For people about to enter, the missing step is their unemployment.

On the hiring market, one self-media account offers a frightening pair of numbers: **front-end job openings down 51% over the past year**; another claims a **40% salary premium for "front-end + AI" skills**. Both come from self-media, and I could not find a cross-checkable primary source, so treat them as "somebody says so" rather than as data: **the trend is credible; the numbers are not yet verified.** The OECD numbers do point the same way from the other side: companies reporting lower staffing needs (9%) do outnumber those reporting higher needs (6%).

## 7. A few objections I expect to get

Articles like this usually draw a few complaints. Here are the four I expect, answered once and for all.

### "Contracting jobs ≠ AI substitution"

Fair, and I'll take it.

The LatePost report contains a qualifier that has to be quoted: **not a single Chinese company has publicly announced layoffs caused by AI substitution**. The reduction has happened through flexible means — not hiring, not renewing contracts, adjusting the business. The timeline in that report reads the same way: Fliggy on 11 June, Shopee on 8 June, and 30% of one Meituan department on 17 June, with **630 (30 June)** as the shared last day.

There are counter-examples too. The reduction on ByteDance's Wuhan team was, by the public account, **mainly business-line contraction** rather than AI directly replacing people.

But put more precisely, "being replaced by AI" may not even be the right description. The Huxiu observation is closer to the truth: **what disappears first is not the old jobs but the new jobs that were never created.** Nobody was "replaced" — they simply found that the position which would have appeared for them did not appear this time. Which is why the statistics show nothing: no layoff lists, no last days, only a slightly larger gap between companies reporting lower staffing needs (9%) and those reporting higher needs (6%).

So this article will not contain a conclusion like "so many front-end jobs were cut". Whether the total is falling and how the structure is changing are two questions, and here I only answer the second one.

### "Most of your data comes from self-media"

The first half is right; I changed the second half.

The hard numbers in the body come from a handful of places: Meituan's two official posts (310,000 lines; 90%+; 117 → 36/3/41/37; 678,000 lines of WARN logs; 31 build files), the LatePost deep dive, Hong Dingkun speaking on the record in Securities Times, The Paper's monthly-visit comparison, and the OECD, ILO and Stanford figures quoted inside the Huxiu piece. You can open all of them and check.

Only two figures come from self-media: front-end openings down 51%, and a 40% salary premium for "front-end + AI". I could not find a cross-checkable primary source, so their place in the text is "somebody says so" — they show up once, in the hiring-market paragraph, as a sign of a trend rather than as an argument.

A number with no source does not belong in this article. That rule applies to me too.

### "Going full stack just means front-end engineers get more work"

That worry is real, and I don't have a neat sentence to wave it away.

The LatePost interviews describe something close to this worry: role boundaries are dissolving, front end is merged into back end, newly posted roles are renamed "full stack". Add the reason one front-end engineer was given for leaving — "the back end can now write front-end code by itself" — and it looks like more work for fewer people.

But Taobao Tmall Technology's March retrospective gives the other half: **AI is markedly efficient at boilerplate code, unit tests and UI components, but still limited on complex business scenarios.** "The back end can write front end" holds at the boilerplate layer and does not hold at the complex-business layer. And the complex-business layer is exactly where people still remain.

There is another LatePost line that answers it: **"The efficiency gains from AI did not turn into leisure. Work that used to take ten days, the boss now expects delivered in one."** If going full stack only means taking on more work, that is a failed version of going full stack. I would judge it by two markers: does business throughput per head rise, and does judgement get outsourced.

If you meet the first condition and lose the second, what you have done is turn yourself into a cheaper execution unit — and that costs you more than staying where you were.

Meituan's A1 conclusion works as the ruler: the centre of gravity moves to **"designing and maintaining an engineering environment in which AI can reliably produce code"**. That role is not "more work"; it is "being responsible for the work being less wrong".

### "The more code AI writes, the easier life gets"

Meituan's A2 answers this in three sentences: **"Fluency is not evidence, consistency is not correctness, green is not resolution"**, and **"AI's mistakes are not in the code, but outside the code"**.

More output does not mean less risk. **Verification cost is the new scarce resource.** A team that writes ten times the code but cannot say why any of it is correct is in far more danger than a team that writes less and can account for every line. The harness post-mortem — "the dashboards stayed green, not one alert fired" — is the best illustration of that judgement: when things break, the alarm does not ring.

One more piece of positive evidence belongs here: the circulating screenshot's line — **"a front-end engineer no longer needs three months to learn how to write a back-end endpoint"**. Falling migration cost is AI's most direct contribution to the individual. It turns "become full stack" from something that took three years of gritted teeth into something you can learn while you work.

## 8. When the job title is gone, where do you stand

Rather than guessing whether front end survives, ask three questions you can actually answer:

1. **Does your team still have a job title?** (organisational layer: Agent engineer / full stack / split by business domain)
2. **Are you still delivering "code"?** (artefact layer: specifications, rules, acceptance criteria)
3. **Who is accountable when it goes wrong?** (responsibility layer: the person who signs cannot be outsourced)

The further right your answer sits on each question, the more secure the role. If a job title is already being renamed at layer 1, no longer delivers code at layer 2, and has no accountable person at layer 3, then it is close to being compressed.

Four concrete things:

**One: productise your judgement.** Learn to write always-on AI Rules, Skills and acceptance scripts, so that conventions move from "suggestions in a document" to "constraints the agent executes every time". This is Meituan's "human-to-machine alignment" (A1) — **if your experience is not written down, it does not exist**.

**Two: treat verification as the main event.** Build the ability to attach an evidence chain to AI output: red tests, log audits, regressions, reproducible defect descriptions. This is the 117 → 36 sequence in Meituan's A2 — **people who can falsify are scarcer than people who can write**. The line about "one cost item people forget: human review" in the Hy4 review is saying the same thing.

**Three: move towards the business domain.** Generic syntax is a public good; business rules are not. The value of Tmall's knowledge base comes entirely from distilled business knowledge. If your experience cannot be written as a business rule, it is still a personal trick, and it will disappear with you.

**Four: move up a level and become the person who calls it.** The first three are about catching the work; the fourth is about owning the responsibility. There is a line in the Huxiu piece I read twice: **"a senior programmer exists because someone allowed them, ten years ago, to write code that was not yet good."** The Stanford numbers point the same way — early-career workers aged 22 to 25 saw roughly a 16% relative employment decline while seniors stayed relatively stable. AI took the first rung of the ladder away and pushed weight onto the top of it. So the safest position is not "writing faster than AI" but **knowing what can be handed to AI, what cannot, and being able to say why**.

And one last note, which I think matters most of all: **turn "I can hold on alone" into "I can get the whole team through it".** The scarce thing in the AI era is not individual throughput; it is people who can **define constraints, distil rules, and carry the final judgement**.

---

## Closing thoughts

Back to the three signals from the opening: Meituan's 90%, the circulating "Agent engineer" screenshot from Alibaba, Tencent's AI-Native session. They point at the same thing: **technical roles are being re-sorted from "classified by skill" to "classified by responsibility".** Under that new classification, "front end" increasingly becomes a label like "fluent in a particular framework" — it describes where you came from, not what you are responsible for.

So the question worth asking is not "does front end still exist". It is: **under the new division of responsibility, is the piece you own something nobody else can take?**

If the answer is "what I do is write the things AI can now write faster than I can", then it does not matter what the role is called — the risk is identical either way. If, on the other hand, you hold **the business rules, the evidence chain, and the final judgement**, then the title is irrelevant: an organisation ultimately needs someone to be accountable for the outcome, and that someone has to be a person.

The last question in the Huxiu piece is "does a person's value have to be proven by a job?". Under the topic of changing roles, it is probably worth asking differently — **when "a job" itself becomes a variable that can be added and removed, what do we still use to prove ourselves?** Probably the same three things: the business rules, the evidence chain, and being willing to put your own name on it when things break.

> **The role shrinks, the value moves. From front-end engineer to Agent engineer, what changes is not the name — it is the range of what you can put your signature on.**
